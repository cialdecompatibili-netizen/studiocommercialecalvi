<p>Conversion, merger and demerger are the three extraordinary transactions by which a company changes legal form or structure without going through liquidation and dissolution. The most important common feature, from the tax point of view, is neutrality: if carried out correctly, they do not generate immediate taxation on latent capital gains.</p><h2 id="conversion-the-form-changes-not-the-identity">Conversion: the form changes, not the identity</h2><p>Conversion changes the company type (from Srl to Spa, from partnership to limited company, or vice versa) while keeping the same legal identity: no new entity is created and there is no universal succession. Tax neutrality is automatic and unconditional, except in the case of heterogeneous conversion (art. 171 TUIR) and the limits on carrying forward past losses (art. 172, paragraph 7 TUIR).</p><h2 id="merger-two-or-more-companies-become-one">Merger: two or more companies become one</h2><p>A merger joins two or more companies into one, by absorption (one company absorbs another) or by union (a new entity is created). The merger plan must be drawn up by each management body involved and filed with the Business Register at least 30 days before the shareholders' resolution — a period the shareholders can waive unanimously, but not the publicity itself.</p><h2 id="demerger-one-company-splits-into-several-entities">Demerger: one company splits into several entities</h2><p>A demerger transfers the entire assets (total demerger) or part of them (partial demerger) to one or more beneficiary companies, existing or newly formed, with the related shares allocated to the shareholders of the demerged company. It is the mirror image of a merger: while a merger unites, a demerger separates business branches or reorganises assets in view of new strategies.</p><h2 id="tax-neutrality-art-173-tuir">Tax neutrality (art. 173 TUIR)</h2><p>Mergers and demergers generate neither realisation nor distribution of gains or losses on the assets transferred — including inventory and goodwill — provided that assets and liabilities keep the same tax value they had in the original company. It is a regime of suspension, not exemption: the tax value "travels" with the assets into the resulting company, and taxation will emerge only when those assets are actually realised.</p><h2 id="beware-of-valid-economic-reasons">Beware of valid economic reasons</h2><p>Tax neutrality presupposes that the transaction's main aim is not fraud or tax evasion, but is justified by real restructuring or rationalisation needs. An extraordinary transaction lacking real economic substance, built only to obtain a tax advantage, can be disregarded by the Revenue Agency.</p><h2 id="fixed-costs-of-the-transactions">Fixed costs of the transactions</h2><p>Registration tax payable at a fixed amount (€200) for mergers, demergers and conversions; mortgage and land registry taxes at a fixed amount (€200 each) when the transaction involves a transfer of property — modest costs compared with the asset value typically at stake in these transactions.</p><h2 id="how-we-assist-with-extraordinary-transactions">How we assist with extraordinary transactions</h2><p>Together with the entrepreneur we assess which transaction best meets the real objective (reorganisation, separation of business branches, entry of new shareholders), prepare the required technical documentation (merger/demerger plan, directors' reports) and check that the transaction keeps all the tax neutrality requirements, avoiding later challenges on the economic genuineness of the transaction.</p>
