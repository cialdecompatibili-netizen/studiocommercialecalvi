<p>When an e-commerce business sells abroad and the goods do not physically pass through the Italian warehouse, but travel directly from a supplier to a customer in another country, this is called a triangulation. It is a common transaction for those doing dropshipping or working with European and non-EU suppliers, but it must be handled with care because VAT obligations change a great deal depending on who the three parties involved are.</p><h2 id="what-a-triangulation-is">What a triangulation is</h2><p>A triangulation always involves three parties: the first supplier (the vendor), the promoter (the one who buys and resells, e.g. your e-commerce business) and the final recipient (the customer). The goods move only once, from the first supplier directly to the final customer, but for tax purposes two separate supplies take place: from the supplier to the promoter and from the promoter to the customer.</p><h2 id="eu-triangulation-3-eu-states">EU triangulation (3 EU States)</h2><p>If the three parties are in three different Member States, the transaction benefits from the VAT non-taxable regime for the Italian promoter, provided everyone has an active VIES number and the intra-Community transport is documented (CMR, DDT). Without this proof, the transaction loses the benefit and risks ordinary taxation.</p><h2 id="non-eu-triangulation">Non-EU triangulation</h2><p>When one of the parties is outside the EU, the rules change. If the non-EU supplier issues an invoice through a tax representative in Italy, the Italian customer must issue a self-invoice using the reverse charge mechanism under art. 17 of Presidential Decree 633/72. If instead a tax representative is appointed in the supplier's EU country, the transaction becomes a true EU triangulation, with an intra-Community purchase for the Italian customer.</p><h2 id="the-specific-case-of-dropshipping">The specific case of dropshipping</h2><p>In dropshipping triangulation is the norm: the online seller holds no stock, receives the order and passes it to the supplier (often in Asia or another EU country), who ships directly to the final customer. Here complexity increases because you must check case by case whether the transaction falls under the OSS regime, a customs triangulation with import into the customer's EU country, or an IOSS regime for low-value non-EU shipments.</p><h2 id="documents-to-always-keep">Documents to always keep</h2><ul><li>Invoices for both supplies (supplier-promoter and promoter-customer)</li><li>Proof of direct transport from the first supplier to the final customer</li><li>VAT/VIES identification numbers of all the parties involved</li><li>Customs export declaration, if the triangulation involves a non-EU country</li></ul><p>A badly set-up triangulation does not just lose the tax benefit: it exposes you to challenges in case of an audit, with the risk of having to pay VAT you thought was not due. It is worth getting support from the structure of the contract with supplier and customer, not just at the time of invoicing.</p>
