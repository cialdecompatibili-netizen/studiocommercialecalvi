<p>When a business closes, the road it takes is not always the same. There is voluntary liquidation, decided by the shareholders when the company is still financially healthy, and there is judicial liquidation, imposed by the court when the business can no longer pay its debts. Knowing the difference helps you understand what to expect and, where possible, to choose the best route in advance.</p><h2 id="voluntary-liquidation-the-shareholders-choice">Voluntary liquidation: the shareholders' choice</h2><p>In voluntary liquidation it is the shareholders who decide to close the business, with a meeting that appoints one or more liquidators. No crisis is needed to start it: it can be a strategic choice, the owner's retirement, the end of an entrepreneurial project or simply the wish not to continue. The liquidator handles the sale of assets, payment of creditors in the order set by law and, if anything is left, distribution of the remaining assets among the shareholders.</p><p>The key point is that the company must be solvent: if during liquidation it emerges that debts exceed what can be realised, the voluntary procedure is no longer enough and the road opens towards the judicial one.</p><h2 id="judicial-liquidation-when-the-court-decides">Judicial liquidation: when the court decides</h2><p>Judicial liquidation (which replaced the old bankruptcy with the Business Crisis Code) opens when the company is insolvent, that is, no longer able to regularly pay its debts. It can be requested by creditors, the debtor itself or the public prosecutor. The court appoints a trustee who takes over management of the assets in place of the entrepreneur, under the supervision of a delegated judge.</p><p>Here the difference from voluntary liquidation is substantial: the entrepreneur loses control of decisions, no longer decides the timing, and creditors are satisfied according to strict equality rules (the so-called par condicio creditorum).</p><h2 id="what-happens-if-a-company-in-voluntary-liquidation-becomes-insolvent">What happens if a company in voluntary liquidation becomes insolvent</h2><p>It is not unusual for a liquidation that began as voluntary to become judicial, if along the way it emerges that the assets do not cover the debts. The most recent case law has clarified that placing a company in voluntary liquidation, even if decided after a creditor has already requested judicial liquidation, is a legitimate act — but it is not enough to save the company if its assets do not truly hold up, not just on paper.</p><p>For this reason, before resolving on a voluntary liquidation it is essential to seriously check the asset position: a liquidation balance sheet that understates debts or overstates assets exposes directors and liquidators to liability.</p><h2 id="the-differences-in-summary">The differences in summary</h2><p>In voluntary liquidation the initiative comes from within (the shareholders), management stays with liquidators chosen by the meeting, and there is none of the reputational stigma typical of insolvency proceedings. In judicial liquidation the initiative comes from outside, management passes to a trustee appointed by the court, and the procedure carries heavier consequences for directors and shareholders, including possible liability actions.</p><h2 id="how-we-can-help">How we can help</h2><p>Before resolving on a liquidation, it is important to make an honest diagnosis of the situation: checking whether debts are truly covered by realisable assets, and not just by balance sheet values. We help you assess the company's real solvency, choose the most suitable procedure and handle all the formalities, from the initial liquidation balance sheet to removal from the Business Register, reducing the risk of later challenges.</p>
