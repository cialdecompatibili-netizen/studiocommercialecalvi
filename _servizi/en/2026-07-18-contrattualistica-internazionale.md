<p>Selling abroad means signing contracts with counterparties subject to another legal system. A contract written with only Italian law in mind, without considering that the other side has a different legal system, risks leaving the company exposed just when a dispute arises. Here is what to always specify in an international contract.</p><h2 id="legge-applicabile-la-clausola-che-decide-le-regole-del-gioco">Governing law: the clause that sets the rules of the game</h2><p>A contract is international when it has links with more than one legal system: a single international element, such as the counterparty being based in another country, is enough to require deciding which law governs the relationship. Without an explicit clause, in a dispute the rules of private international law apply (in the EU, the Rome I Regulation), which can lead to a result different from what the company would have wanted. It is therefore best to always state, clearly and in coordination with the other clauses, which national law governs the contract — and if it falls within international trade in goods, to consider whether to expressly include or exclude the Vienna Convention on the international sale of goods (CISG), which otherwise applies automatically in many cases.</p><h2 id="foro-competente-o-arbitrato-dove-si-risolve-una-controversia">Jurisdiction or arbitration: where a dispute is settled</h2><p>Equally important is deciding who judges in the event of a dispute. The parties can choose a specific state court (exclusively, so as to prevent the case being heard before an inconvenient foreign court) or refer the matter to private arbitrators through an arbitration clause. If neither a court nor arbitration is chosen, jurisdiction will be determined under the general rules of international law — often to the disadvantage of the party that did not negotiate the clause carefully. You should also check in advance whether a judgment obtained in the chosen court will actually be enforceable in the counterparty's country: winning a case that then cannot be enforced is a real risk to assess before signing.</p><h2 id="le-altre-clausole-da-non-dimenticare">Other clauses not to forget</h2><p>Besides governing law and jurisdiction, a well-written international contract typically includes: a confidentiality clause to protect sensitive information and trade secrets; a force majeure clause governing what happens in the event of extraordinary events (customs blockages, natural disasters, geopolitical crises) that make performance impossible or too burdensome; indemnity and hold-harmless clauses that decide in advance who bears the risks towards third parties; and a termination clause that defines notice periods and any penalties.</p><h2 id="come-possiamo-aiutarti">How we can help</h2><p>We help you build international contracts tailored to your sector and to the counterparty's country, checking that governing law, jurisdiction and the ancillary clauses are consistent with each other and that the contract is truly enforceable — not just well written on paper.</p>
