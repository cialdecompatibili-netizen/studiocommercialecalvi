<p>When a business operates in several countries, the underlying tax question is always the same: who taxes what, and where? Cross-border tax planning serves to answer in a structured way, avoiding both double taxation and the equally real risk of challenges for non-genuine tax residence.</p><h2 id="the-principle-of-worldwide-taxation">The principle of worldwide taxation</h2><p>Anyone who is tax resident in a country is taxed there on all income wherever produced, not just on income generated in that country. A company's tax residence is determined by criteria such as registered office, the place where strategic decisions are taken and the presence of a permanent establishment abroad. For individuals, what counts is elements such as physical presence (the day count), personal domicile and the centre of vital interests.</p><h2 id="permanent-establishment-when-a-foreign-business-is-also-taxed-in-italy">Permanent establishment: when a foreign business is also taxed in Italy</h2><p>A non-resident business becomes subject to tax in another State when it has a permanent establishment there: a physical or functional presence exceeding a certain threshold of stability and substance. Recent changes to the OECD Model Convention have broadened this definition, adding anti-fragmentation provisions designed to prevent a group from artificially splitting its activities across several entities just to stay below the threshold. Assessing in advance whether an activity abroad creates a permanent establishment is a step to take before starting operations, not after an assessment.</p><h2 id="how-double-taxation-is-avoided">How double taxation is avoided</h2><p>Bilateral double taxation treaties set out which State has the right to tax a given income, and provide two main mechanisms to prevent the same income being taxed twice: the tax credit (the State of residence grants a credit for tax already paid abroad) or exemption of foreign income from tax in the State of residence. Without an applicable treaty, or in case of dual tax residence, the situation becomes more complicated and may require a mutual agreement procedure (MAP) between the tax authorities involved.</p><h2 id="substance-matters-more-than-form">Substance matters more than form</h2><p>Tax authorities increasingly look at the real economic substance of transactions, not just formal aspects: where management is located, where key staff work, where the main assets are located. In an audit, what is examined includes board minutes, delegations of authority, contracts, banking relationships, travel logs and even IT logs. A foreign structure without real substance risks a challenge for exterior residence (esterovestizione), with resulting reclassifications, penalties and de facto double taxation.</p><h2 id="how-we-can-help">How we can help</h2><p>We help you build solid cross-border tax planning: checking tax residence and permanent establishment risk, correct application of double taxation treaties, and documentation of economic substance that holds up to an audit, not just on paper.</p>
